Actions Carry Implicit Information

In a multiagent system, the actions of other agents carry implicit information. Each agent responds to what it observes, knows, and expects. By observing its behavior, another agent can learn about parts of the environment it cannot observe directly.

1. Road hazard warnings from other drivers

These two videos connect the idea to a practical application: a navigation network that uses traffic information to warn drivers about hazards beyond their direct line of sight.

The application: connected navigation

According to the official launch announcement, Eagle Eye Guardian analyses anonymized vehicle location, speed, and acceleration data. Abrupt slowing or braking by several vehicles can indicate an incident ahead, allowing the system to send warnings to approaching drivers.

The connection to implicit information is that these drivers are already reacting to road conditions. Their actions become evidence about those conditions. A navigation service can combine that evidence across vehicles and turn it into an explicit warning for someone who has not yet encountered the hazard.

2. Inferring a hidden traffic light from traffic behavior

Imagine approaching an intersection where a truck or glare blocks the traffic light. Vehicles in the same lane begin to move, while traffic on a conflicting approach comes to a stop. Those actions provide evidence that the signal for your direction may have turned green, even though the light itself remains hidden.

The inference works because the light influences other drivers’ decisions. Observing those decisions lets an agent reason backwards about the light. It remains uncertain: a vehicle may stop for congestion or a pedestrian, and drivers can make mistakes. Lane geometry, movements over time, and other observations help distinguish these explanations.

The application: Gaode’s traffic-light countdowns

Gaode (Amap) has already developed this into an established navigation feature: estimating the remaining red-light time and the number of signal cycles a driver may need to wait. A 2022 product update from vivo’s Jovi InCar team documented support across nearly 240 cities and more than 80,000 intersections.

The inference principle appears in AutoNavi’s patent on estimating traffic-light cycles: collect trajectories of vehicles waiting at a signal, compare when they start moving, and extract recurring timing patterns to estimate the signal cycle. Drivers’ stop-and-go behavior carries information about the light that a navigation system can recover.

Gaode has also partnered with local traffic authorities to incorporate official signal data. The implicit-information example is specifically the part that reconstructs timing from vehicle behavior.

Supporting research and presentation

Agent-Aware State Estimation in Autonomous Vehicles, by Shane Parr, Ishan Khatri, Justin Svegliato, and Shlomo Zilberstein (IROS 2021), directly studies this problem. It combines observations of other vehicles with a probabilistic model to estimate obscured traffic lights. Evaluated on real driving data with simulated occlusions, it outperformed a baseline that used only traffic-light observations.

The authors’ presentation illustrates the same idea: stopped and moving vehicles can help reveal a light’s state when direct vision fails. The example supports using other agents’ behavior as an additional source of evidence in perception.
