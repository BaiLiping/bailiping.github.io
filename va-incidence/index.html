Interactive note · multipath geometry for radio SLAM

One multipath measurement, two geometries: the virtual anchor and the incidence point

A reflected radio path hands you three numbers: a delay (a path length), an angle, and a pathloss. Read the delay and angle one way — walk the full measured length along the arrival direction — and you get a virtual anchor: a fictitious, wall-agnostic transmitter that BP-SLAM treats as a static feature. Read them the other way — stop where the arrival ray crosses the delay ellipse whose foci are the BS and the UE — and you get the incidence point: the physical spot on the wall where the ray actually bounced. Same data, two maps.

Context. The inputs mirror the per-path estimates (delay, AoA/AoD, amplitude) produced by the SAGE estimator in the Gaussian_Splatting_Test pipeline. The virtual-anchor reading is the feature model of E. Leitinger et al., “A Belief Propagation Algorithm for Multipath-Based SLAM,” IEEE Trans. Wireless Commun. 18(12), 2019; the anchor-mirroring idea goes back to Channel-SLAM (Gentner et al., IEEE TWC 15(9), 2016). Scene and numbers here are illustrative (scale: 1 px ≙ 0.1 m).

01What a single path actually tells you

For one multipath component the channel estimator (SAGE, ESPRIT, …) returns a triple. Each element has its own geometric meaning:

delay τ → a length L = cτ : the locus ‖x − BS‖ + ‖x − UE‖ = L is an ellipse with foci BS and UE — every point that could have produced this delay by a single bounce.
angle φ (AoA at the UE) : a ray out of the UE — the direction the energy came from. With arrays at both ends there is a twin, ψ (AoD at the BS): a ray out of the BS — the direction the energy left in.
pathloss → a weight : how much energy survived; it does not move any geometry, it says how much to trust / how brightly to paint whatever the geometry produces.

The ray and the ellipse intersect in exactly one point on the UE side — that intersection is the incidence point. Walking the full length L along the same ray lands on the virtual anchor. In both demos below, the drawing is constructed from the data: the orange sliders are the measurement — delay τ·c, AoA φ at the UE, AoD ψ at the BS — and every curve and marker follows from them. Drag the UE and the sliders re-sync to what that UE would actually measure; nudge a slider and you inject estimation error. And the ellipses are not just displayed — press the ✏ trace buttons to watch each one drawn by its definition: a string of length L pinned at the two foci, kept taut by the moving pencil, the two segment lengths always summing to L.

02Single bounce: draw everything from (τ, φ, ψ)

One wall — but the receiver does not know it. From the data alone: the delay draws the ellipse, the angle draws the ray, their intersection is the incidence point P, the full-length walk is the VA — and the wall itself is inferred as the perpendicular bisector of BS↔VA through P. (The wall is tilted on purpose: AoA and AoD are tied only through the reflection law — equal angles about the wall normal — so in global coordinates they are generally different numbers; an axis-aligned wall would misleadingly make them mirror images.) With clean data the inferred wall lands exactly on the true wall (faint grey). The AoD ψ measured at the BS is data too, and it over-determines P: its ray must cross the delay ellipse at the same point the AoA ray does. Perturb τ, φ or ψ with the sliders: the ellipse breathes, P slides, the inferred wall tilts — and when the two angle estimates disagree, a second hollow point P(ψ) splits off from P(φ). That gap is the bistatic consistency residual an estimator gates on.

03Double bounce: two virtual anchors, two incidence points

Now the path bounces twice: BS → P₁ (wall A) → P₂ (wall B) → UE. Mirroring composes: VA¹ = BS mirrored in wall A (the first-bounce anchor), VA² = VA¹ mirrored in wall B. The UE still sees a clean LOS-like anchor — ‖UE − VA²‖ = L₂ and the AoA ray points at VA² — which is exactly why BP-SLAM can treat second-order paths as just another static feature. The incidence points need care, because the receiver must first guess the bounce order. Hypothesis 1 — a single bounce: pin the string at BS and UE (red). One bounce must then absorb the entire measured length — the full BS → wall A → wall B → UE trip — so its bounce point lands beyond wall B (the two red distances sum to the full L₂), on an implied wall of its own, tilted through the corner. Perfectly coherent — but that wall matches neither wall A nor wall B, and that is how the hypothesis dies. Hypothesis 2 — double bounce off A then B: pin the string at VA¹ and the UE — the ellipse with the same measured L₂ passes exactly through P₂. The first bounce then gets an ellipse of its own (green): peel off the direct leg ‖UE→P₂‖ from the string — what remains is the length of the BS → P₁ → P₂ sub-trip, a single-bounce path between BS and P₂, so the leftover string pinned at BS and P₂ traces an ellipse through P₁. And the angle that picks P₁ off that ellipse is the path’s other measured angle: the AoD at the BS — a MIMO sounder measures each path at both ends, and bounce 1 is seen from the BS exactly as bounce 2 is seen from the UE. (With a map instead, you could aim from P₂ at VA¹; the two constructions agree exactly when data and map are consistent.) Be precise about what is data and what is map, though: (τ, φ, ψ) alone do not pin the split — slide P₁ outward along the AoD ray while P₂ slides inward along the AoA ray and the total length stays L₂, a one-parameter family of explanations. The single piece of map knowledge in this construction is VA¹: it selects P₂ on the AoA ray, and everything else follows. A single bounce needs no map at all; every extra bounce costs one anchor of map knowledge — and here that anchor is earned in-scene, not assumed: wall A also returns a single-bounce path (path 1, orange), whose measured (τ⁽¹⁾, φ⁽¹⁾) bootstrap VA¹ by the full-length walk before the double-bounce machinery runs. Two measured paths, five numbers, no prior map. That recursion is the general recipe: strip the last leg, and an n-bounce problem becomes an (n−1)-bounce problem one wall earlier. The ✏ trace draws the two-bounce construction in propagation order — the bounce-1 segment ellipse through P₁ first, then the bounce-2 ellipse through P₂. Note that the virtual anchor is blind to this choice: walking the full L₂ lands on the same VA under either hypothesis — bounce order is a question only the incidence-point reading must answer, and the map is what answers it. Here too the drawing runs on the data: perturb τ, φ or ψ and the estimated points slide off the walls while the diffuse clouds stay on the true surface. (Why is wall B tilted 20° off perpendicular? Because exactly perpendicular walls form a corner retroreflector: the double bounce returns every ray anti-parallel to how it left, forcing the measured AoD to equal the AoA for any UE position. The tilt is what lets the two measured angles differ.) Slide the roughness up to let the wall scatter: the specular points diffuse into clouds of reflection points, and the once point-like VA² smears into a cloud too.

04Double bounce, corridor edition: two parallel walls

Same machinery, opposite degeneracy. Put the UE between two parallel walls with the BS down the corridor: the double bounce zig-zags off wall R, then wall L. Two reflections in parallel walls compose to a pure translation, so the first and last legs are exactly parallel — the measured AoD is locked 180° from the measured AoA no matter where the UE stands (the perpendicular corner locked them equal; the corridor locks them opposite). And the image sources march sideways: VA¹ sits one mirror-step to the right, VA² two corridor-widths to the left — the first rungs of the classic image-source ladder that higher bounce orders continue. The three ellipses need no new ideas: same measured L₂, same three pinnings — BS & UE (1-bounce hypothesis), VA¹ & UE (bounce 2), BS & P₂ with the leftover string (bounce 1).

05Estimating the map: motion collapses the family

The double-bounce constructions above spent one anchor of map knowledge, VA¹ — but in SLAM the map is unknown; VA¹ is the thing to estimate. Two mechanisms deliver it. Bootstrap up the ladder: single-bounce paths are map-free — every snapshot of demo 02 yields a wall and its VA from data alone, and once wall A is mapped, VA¹ exists and double-bounce paths become interpretable (each of which then maps wall B): order-n paths become usable exactly when the order-(n−1) anchors exist — the image-source ladder is also the estimation ladder. (The orange layer in the demo draws exactly this: the same wall A also returns a single-bounce path, and walking its full measured length along its own AoA ray lands on VA¹ directly — one clean order-1 path does the work of a second pose.) Motion kills the leftover ambiguity: even perfect (τ, φ, ψ) leave the one-parameter family of bounce-point splits at a single pose. But the walls are static. Every family member implies a wall at P₁, hence a VA¹ candidate — so each pose draws a curve of possible maps. Curves from different poses must agree, and they can only agree at the true VA¹. One pose = a curve of maps; two poses = a point. Slide the family parameter and watch the candidate travel along pose a’s curve until it hits pose b’s — that crossing is the map. (In reality the associations and bounce orders are unknown too, so all of this runs inside probabilistic machinery — BP data association over VA features in BP-SLAM, or one joint posterior over trajectory, map and associations, the theme of the JVS-SLAM note.)

06Why the diffuse case decides the representation

With smooth walls the two readings are equivalent bookkeeping. Rough walls break the tie. A diffuse surface returns energy from a whole patch, not a point: the measurement becomes a family of slightly different (τ, φ, PL) triples. Map those into incidence points and you get a weighted point cloud hugging the physical wall — each point carrying its pathloss as a weight. Exactly the kind of data a splat-style surface representation wants (this is what the point-cloud view in Gaussian_Splatting_Test exports, and what the SAGE bistatic triangulation estimates). Map the same family into virtual anchors and the crisp mirror-image anchor smears into a blob that no longer behaves like a point feature — the VA cloud in the demo above.

The one-liner. The VA answers “where do I hear a phantom transmitter?” — great for localizing the UE. The incidence point answers “where is the physical wall?” — great for mapping. Delay + angle feed both; pathloss weights both.
