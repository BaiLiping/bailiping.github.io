One multipath measurement, two geometries: the virtual anchor and the incidence point

A reflected radio path hands you four numbers: a delay (a path length), an arrival angle at the UE, a departure angle at the BS, and a pathloss. Read the delay and angle one way — walk the full measured length along the arrival direction — and you get a virtual anchor: a fictitious, wall-agnostic transmitter that BP-SLAM treats as a static feature. Read them the other way — stop where the arrival ray crosses the delay ellipse whose foci are the BS and the UE — and you get the incidence point: the physical spot on the wall where the ray actually bounced. Same data, two maps.

Context. The inputs mirror the per-path estimates (delay, AoA/AoD, amplitude) produced by the SAGE estimator in the Gaussian_Splatting_Test pipeline. The virtual-anchor reading is the feature model of E. Leitinger et al., “A Belief Propagation Algorithm for Multipath-Based SLAM,” IEEE Trans. Wireless Commun. 18(12), 2019; the anchor-mirroring idea goes back to Channel-SLAM (Gentner et al., IEEE TWC 15(9), 2016). Scene and numbers here are illustrative (scale: 1 px ≙ 0.1 m).

01Measurement

For one multipath component the channel estimator (SAGE, ESPRIT, …) returns a tuple (τ, φ, ψ, PL). Each element has its own geometric meaning:

delay τ → a length L = cτ
angle φ (AoA at the UE)
angle ψ (AoD at the BS)
pathloss → a weight

Motion, quantified. The UE walks t₁ → t₄. One pose constrains VA¹ to a curve; the second pose’s curve intersects it in a point (teal ring, found numerically — not read off the ground truth); every further pose must pass through the same point. That over-consistency across motion is what makes VA¹ a reliable static feature to hang SLAM on.

03Optimization view with unknown UE position

3.1Estimating the UE: change the foci, bootstrap from the BS side

Every construction so far pinned a string at the UE — the delay ellipse used foci (BS, UE), the bounce-2 ellipse (VA¹, UE). In SLAM the UE is the other unknown: those pins do not exist yet. But the BS-side pin survives, and the string can be re-hung from it. For a single-bounce path, put the bounce on the departure ray — P₁ = BS + t·d(ψ) at an unknown walk t — and make the leftover string reach the UE against the measured arrival direction: p(t) = BS + t·d − (L−t)·u. Every t is a coherent explanation (a UE, a bounce point, and the wall that bisects them), and as t slides the candidate UE traces a straight line. The line even has a name: d + u is parallel to the reflecting wall's normal (the mirror flips the normal component of the direction and keeps the tangential one, so the tangential parts of d and u cancel) — each path confines the unknown UE to a line through the true UE along its last wall's normal; sliding along it just re-prices how far away that wall is. One path = one line, no map needed. Two single-bounce paths off differently-oriented walls = two transversal lines = the UE — and each path's t then falls out, so both bounce points, both walls, and both VAs are earned in the same stroke: §2.6's map ladder and this UE bootstrap are the same recursion run from opposite ends. Higher orders continue by changing the foci: once wall A is known, strip the double-bounce path from the BS side — P₁ = AoD ray ∩ wall A is now a known point; reflect the ray in wall A (equivalently, re-pin the string at VA¹ instead of BS) and the remainder is a single-bounce problem sourced at P₁, contributing its own candidate line. Note where that line points: along wall B's normal — the same line the order-1 path off wall B already drew. The candidate line depends only on the path's last wall, so a higher-order path adds UE information exactly when its last wall is one no lower-order path reaches. LOS is the degenerate rung — no wall, no leftover freedom: p = BS + L·d outright. (Two caveats: axes here are world-aligned; an unknown UE heading rotates every measured φ by one shared unknown, still solvable with a third path. And this is per-snapshot geometry — a real estimator fuses these lines through motion, noise, and association machinery.)

Read it. The slider walks t out along path a's departure ray: P₁(t) slides on the ray, the leftover string L−t hangs from it against the arrival direction, and the candidate UE(t) glides along the straight orange line — perpendicular to wall A, because every candidate's implied wall (dark segment, the bisector at P₁) stays parallel to the true one. Path b draws the same construction off wall B: a second line, crossed at 70°, and the crossing is the UE — no prior map, no known start. Layer c strips the double-bounce path at the bootstrapped wall A (the string re-pinned at VA¹/P₁ — the changed foci) and lands exactly on line b: a confirmation, not new information, because a candidate-UE line remembers only the path's last wall. With jittered data the three lines miss each other by the noise scale and the least-squares crossing moves ~0.3 m — the graceful, transversal failure mode the tangential VA-family of §2.6 never had.

04Why the diffuse case decides the representation

With smooth walls the two readings are equivalent bookkeeping. Rough walls break the tie. A diffuse surface returns energy from a whole patch, not a point: the measurement becomes a family of slightly different (τ, φ, ψ, PL) tuples. Map those into incidence points and you get a weighted point cloud hugging the physical wall — each point carrying its pathloss as a weight. Exactly the kind of data a splat-style surface representation wants (this is what the point-cloud view in Gaussian_Splatting_Test exports, and what the SAGE bistatic triangulation estimates). Map the same family into virtual anchors and the crisp mirror-image anchor smears into a blob that no longer behaves like a point feature — the VA cloud in the demo above.

The one-liner. The VA answers “where do I hear a phantom transmitter?” — great for localizing the UE. The incidence point answers “where is the physical wall?” — great for mapping. Delay + angle feed both; pathloss weights both.
