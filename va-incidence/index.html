Interactive note · multipath geometry for radio SLAM

One multipath measurement, two geometries: the virtual anchor and the incidence point

A reflected radio path hands you three numbers: a delay (a path length), an angle, and a pathloss. Read the delay and angle one way — walk the full measured length along the arrival direction — and you get a virtual anchor: a fictitious, wall-agnostic transmitter that BP-SLAM treats as a static feature. Read them the other way — stop where the arrival ray crosses the delay ellipse whose foci are the BS and the UE — and you get the incidence point: the physical spot on the wall where the ray actually bounced. Same data, two maps.

Context. The inputs mirror the per-path estimates (delay, AoA/AoD, amplitude) produced by the SAGE estimator in the Gaussian_Splatting_Test pipeline. The virtual-anchor reading is the feature model of E. Leitinger et al., “A Belief Propagation Algorithm for Multipath-Based SLAM,” IEEE Trans. Wireless Commun. 18(12), 2019; the anchor-mirroring idea goes back to Channel-SLAM (Gentner et al., IEEE TWC 15(9), 2016). Scene and numbers here are illustrative (scale: 1 px ≙ 0.1 m).

01What a single path actually tells you

For one multipath component the channel estimator (SAGE, ESPRIT, …) returns a triple. Each element has its own geometric meaning:

delay τ → a length L = cτ : the locus ‖x − BS‖ + ‖x − UE‖ = L is an ellipse with foci BS and UE — every point that could have produced this delay by a single bounce.
angle φ (AoA at the UE) : a ray out of the UE — the direction the energy came from. With arrays at both ends there is a twin, ψ (AoD at the BS): a ray out of the BS — the direction the energy left in.
pathloss → a weight : how much energy survived; it does not move any geometry, it says how much to trust / how brightly to paint whatever the geometry produces.

The ray and the ellipse intersect in exactly one point on the UE side — that intersection is the incidence point. Walking the full length L along the same ray lands on the virtual anchor. In both demos below, the drawing is constructed from the data: the two orange sliders are the measurement (τ·c and φ), and every curve and marker follows from them. Drag the UE and the sliders re-sync to what that UE would actually measure; nudge a slider and you inject estimation error. And the ellipses are not just displayed — press the ✏ trace buttons to watch each one drawn by its definition: a string of length L pinned at the two foci, kept taut by the moving pencil, the two segment lengths always summing to L.

02Single bounce: draw everything from (τ, φ)

One wall — but the receiver does not know it. From the data alone: the delay draws the ellipse, the angle draws the ray, their intersection is the incidence point P, the full-length walk is the VA — and the wall itself is inferred as the perpendicular bisector of BS↔VA through P. With clean data the inferred wall lands exactly on the true wall (faint grey). Now perturb τ or φ with the sliders: watch the ellipse breathe, P slide, and the inferred wall tilt away from the truth — measurement error becoming map error.

03Double bounce: two virtual anchors, two incidence points

Now the path bounces twice: BS → P₁ (wall A) → P₂ (wall B) → UE. Mirroring composes: VA¹ = BS mirrored in wall A (the first-bounce anchor), VA² = VA¹ mirrored in wall B. The UE still sees a clean LOS-like anchor — ‖UE − VA²‖ = L₂ and the AoA ray points at VA² — which is exactly why BP-SLAM can treat second-order paths as just another static feature. The incidence points need care, because the receiver must first guess the bounce order. Hypothesis 1 — a single bounce: pin the string at BS and UE (red). One bounce must then absorb the entire measured length — the full BS → wall A → wall B → UE trip — so its bounce point lands beyond wall B (the two red distances sum to the full L₂), on an implied wall of its own, tilted through the corner. Perfectly coherent — but that wall matches neither wall A nor wall B, and that is how the hypothesis dies. Hypothesis 2 — double bounce off A then B: pin the string at VA¹ and the UE — the ellipse with the same measured L₂ passes exactly through P₂. The first bounce then gets an ellipse of its own (green): peel off the direct leg ‖UE→P₂‖ from the string — what remains is the length of the BS → P₁ → P₂ sub-trip, a single-bounce path between BS and P₂, so the leftover string pinned at BS and P₂ traces an ellipse through P₁. And the angle that picks P₁ off that ellipse is the path’s other measured angle: the AoD at the BS — a MIMO sounder measures each path at both ends, and bounce 1 is seen from the BS exactly as bounce 2 is seen from the UE. (With a map instead, you could aim from P₂ at VA¹; the two constructions agree exactly when data and map are consistent.) That recursion is the general recipe: strip the last leg, and an n-bounce problem becomes an (n−1)-bounce problem one wall earlier. The ✏ trace draws the two-bounce construction in propagation order — the bounce-1 segment ellipse through P₁ first, then the bounce-2 ellipse through P₂. Note that the virtual anchor is blind to this choice: walking the full L₂ lands on the same VA under either hypothesis — bounce order is a question only the incidence-point reading must answer, and the map is what answers it. Here too the drawing runs on the data: perturb τ or φ and the estimated P₂ slides off the wall while the diffuse clouds stay on the true surface. Slide the roughness up to let the wall scatter: the specular points diffuse into clouds of reflection points, and the once point-like VA² smears into a cloud too.

04Why the diffuse case decides the representation

With smooth walls the two readings are equivalent bookkeeping. Rough walls break the tie. A diffuse surface returns energy from a whole patch, not a point: the measurement becomes a family of slightly different (τ, φ, PL) triples. Map those into incidence points and you get a weighted point cloud hugging the physical wall — each point carrying its pathloss as a weight. Exactly the kind of data a splat-style surface representation wants (this is what the point-cloud view in Gaussian_Splatting_Test exports, and what the SAGE bistatic triangulation estimates). Map the same family into virtual anchors and the crisp mirror-image anchor smears into a blob that no longer behaves like a point feature — the VA cloud in the demo above.

The one-liner. The VA answers “where do I hear a phantom transmitter?” — great for localizing the UE. The incidence point answers “where is the physical wall?” — great for mapping. Delay + angle feed both; pathloss weights both.
